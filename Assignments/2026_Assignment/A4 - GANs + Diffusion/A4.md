# A4: Generative Models — Teaching Guide

A walkthrough of GAN → DCGAN → DDPM with the story behind each.

---

## Opening: The Core Problem

**What to say:**

> "Every model we've seen so far is discriminative — given an image, predict a label. Today we flip the problem: given nothing, generate a realistic image. That's generative modeling."

**The fundamental difficulty:**

> "A 32×32 RGB image has 3,072 pixel values. The space of all possible images is astronomically large. But the subset that looks like a real photograph is a tiny, complicated manifold inside that space. We need to learn to sample from that manifold."

Three families, three different strategies:

| Approach | Core Idea | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GAN** | Adversarial game: generator vs discriminator | Fast inference, sharp images | Unstable training, mode collapse |
| **VAE** | Encode to latent, decode back | Stable, smooth latent space | Blurry outputs |
| **Diffusion** | Iteratively denoise from noise | High quality, diverse | Slow sampling (1000 steps) |

## Part 1: GAN (Cells 3–7)

### The story (tell this before any code)

**What to say:**

> "In 2014, Ian Goodfellow had an insight at a bar. What if instead of designing a loss function by hand, you let a neural network learn the loss? That's the GAN idea."

**The minimax game:**

```
Generator G:      noise z → fake image
Discriminator D:  image → real or fake?
```

$$\min_G \max_D \; \mathbb{E}_{x}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]$$

**Intuition to give:**

> "D wants to output 1 for real, 0 for fake — maximize both log terms. G wants D to output 1 for its fakes — minimize log(1 − D(G(z))). They're playing a game where both get better by competing."

**The balance problem:**

> "If D is too strong, it always outputs ~0 for fakes. The gradient of log(1 − D(G(z))) → 0. G has no signal to improve. They must stay roughly matched throughout training. This is the hardest part of GAN training."

**Mode collapse:**

> "G discovers a shortcut: if it always generates the digit '1', D struggles to say that's fake because '1' is a real digit. So G just outputs '1' forever. The generator has found a mode of the distribution and is exploiting it instead of covering the full distribution."

**Pause and ask:**

> "If G collapses to always generating '1', what does D learn to do? And then what does G do?"
>
> Answer: D learns to say '1' is fake. Then G switches to '2'. Then D adapts. They chase each other — this is the GAN training instability spiral.

## Part 2: DCGAN (Cells 8–10)

### The story

**What to say:**

> "Vanilla GAN worked on MNIST but produced blurry noise on real images. Radford et al. (2015) made three changes that made all the difference."

**DCGAN's three rules:**

1. **Replace all pooling with strided convolutions** — let the network learn its own downsampling
2. **Batch Normalization in both G and D** — stabilizes gradients, prevents mode collapse
3. **No fully-connected layers** — convolutions are translationally equivariant, suited to images

**Generator flow:**

```
z (100-dim) → FC → reshape (4×4×512) → ConvTranspose → 8×8 → 16×16 → 32×32 → tanh
```

**What to say about transposed convolutions:**

> "ConvTranspose2d is the learnable upsampling operation. It's not literally the transpose of a convolution — it's a learnable way to go from a small feature map to a larger one. Think of it as 'inverse strided convolution.'"

## Part 3: DDPM (Cells 11–19)

### The story

**What to say:**

> "By 2020, GANs had produced stunning results — but they were famously finicky to train. Mode collapse, vanishing gradients, hyperparameter sensitivity. Ho et al. asked: what if we took inspiration from thermodynamics instead of game theory?"

### The two-process intuition

**Forward process (fixed, not learned):**

> "Take a real image. Add a tiny amount of Gaussian noise. Repeat 1000 times. At the end, you have pure noise — the image is completely destroyed. This is just math, no learning involved."

```
x₀ (clean) → x₁ → x₂ → ... → x₁₀₀₀ (pure noise)
              +ε    +ε          +ε
```

**Reverse process (learned):**

> "Can a neural network learn to undo one step of noising? If it can do that, we can chain 1000 reverse steps: start from pure noise and gradually recover a clean image."

**The shortcut formula — explain this carefully:**

$$x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\, \epsilon$$

> "We don't need to run 1000 forward steps during training. This formula jumps directly to any timestep t from the clean image. $\bar{\alpha}_t$ tells you how much signal is left — at t=0 it's 1 (clean image), at t=T it's ~0 (pure noise)."

**Training objective:**

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \epsilon}\left[ \| \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \|^2 \right]$$

> "We randomly pick a timestep, add the right amount of noise using the shortcut, then ask the U-Net: what noise did we add? MSE between predicted noise and actual noise. That's the entire training loss. No adversarial game."

**Pause and ask:**

> "Why do we predict noise ε instead of predicting the clean image x₀ directly?"
>
> Answer: Both are mathematically equivalent — you can derive one from the other. But predicting noise is better empirically: it's a zero-mean signal, easier to learn, and the network gets a consistent target across all timesteps.

### The U-Net as denoiser (Cell 13)

**What to say:**

> "The denoiser takes two inputs: the noisy image AND the timestep t. Why the timestep? At t=999 (almost pure noise), the network should make big corrections. At t=1 (almost clean), tiny corrections. The timestep tells the network how confident to be."

**Timestep embedding:**

> "The timestep t is encoded with sinusoidal embeddings — same idea as positional encoding in Transformers. Then injected into every U-Net block via addition. The network literally conditions its denoising on how noisy the input is."

### Sampling — why it's slow (Cell 17)

> "To generate one image, we run the U-Net 1000 times. Each run is cheap — but 1000 runs is slow. This is DDPM's main weakness. DDIM (Song et al., 2020) reduces this to 50 steps with the same quality. That's why modern diffusion models are practical."

### Noise schedule (part of Cell 11)

> "The β schedule controls how fast we destroy the image. Linear schedule: β grows uniformly. Cosine schedule (Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021): β grows slowly at first, faster later. The cosine schedule preserves more signal in early steps — the network gets more informative training examples."

## The Big Picture — GAN vs Diffusion

**What to say at the end:**

> "GANs and diffusion models both generate images — but they're philosophically opposite. GANs: implicit density, adversarial, one-shot generation. Diffusion: explicit density, no adversary, iterative refinement."

| | GAN | DDPM |
|---|---|---|
| **Training** | Adversarial (unstable) | MSE regression (stable) |
| **Inference** | Single forward pass (~ms) | 1000 U-Net passes (~seconds) |
| **Mode coverage** | Can collapse | Covers full distribution |
| **Quality** | Sharp but artifacts | High quality, diverse |
| **Controllability** | Hard to condition | Easy (text, class, etc.) |

**Why diffusion won:**

> "Stable Diffusion, DALL-E 2, Midjourney — all diffusion. Not because diffusion is faster (it isn't), but because it's trainable at scale without instability. You can throw more compute at it and it reliably gets better. GANs plateau."

**The connection to Stable Diffusion:**

> "DDPM runs diffusion in pixel space — slow and memory-heavy for high-res images. Rombach et al. (2022) had the insight: first compress the image to a small latent with a VAE (VAE from A3 background), then run diffusion in that latent space. 8× smaller → 64× fewer pixels → practical. That's Latent Diffusion = Stable Diffusion."

## Exercise Walkthrough Tips

**Ex1 (Mode collapse):** Make sure students visualize the class distribution histogram. A collapsed GAN will show 1–2 bars dominating. Connect to Ex1c — the two main fixes are minibatch discrimination and Wasserstein loss (WGAN).

**Ex2 (Noise schedule):** The key observation: linear schedule destroys too much signal in early steps — most training examples are near-pure noise where the target is easy but uninformative. Cosine schedule gives more training examples in the "interesting" middle range.

**Ex3 (CIFAR-10 DDPM):** Color images are much harder. Students will likely need more epochs. The bottleneck is the U-Net forward pass — 3 channels vs 1 doesn't change much, but the network needs more capacity (wider channels).

**Ex4 (GAN vs Diffusion comparison):** The inference time difference will be dramatic — GAN generates 64 images in milliseconds, DDPM takes seconds. This is the practical tradeoff students should internalize.

## Closing Question

> "You're building a real-time avatar generation system for a video game — generate a unique face for each new player, must run in under 100ms. Which architecture?"
>
> Answer: GAN (StyleGAN). Diffusion is too slow for real-time. GAN's single forward pass is milliseconds. Quality is good enough for game avatars. Mode collapse is less of a concern when you want diverse but not exhaustive coverage.

> "Now you're generating medical report images for radiologist training — must cover rare pathologies, quality must be indistinguishable from real scans. Which architecture?"
>
> Answer: Diffusion. Mode coverage is critical (can't miss rare cases). Quality matters more than speed. Stable training at scale.
